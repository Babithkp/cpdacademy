Advance Care Planning

Advance care planning is the process of discussing and recording a person's wishes for their future care, ideally while they still have the capacity to make these decisions. This can include:

  • Where they would like to be cared for and, if possible, to die (home, hospice, care home or hospital).
  • Treatments they would or would not want, such as resuscitation.
  • Who they would like involved in decisions on their behalf.
  • Any cultural, religious or personal wishes about their care.

These wishes are usually recorded in a care plan, and in some areas summarised in a document such as a ReSPECT (Recommended Summary Plan for Emergency Care and Treatment) form, so that all staff involved in the person's care are aware of them.

Building a Person-Centred Care Plan

A good end-of-life care plan is built around the individual, not a standard checklist. It should:

  • Reflect the person's own priorities and preferences, not assumptions made on their behalf.
  • Be developed with the person and their family wherever possible.
  • Be reviewed regularly, as needs can change quickly.
  • Be shared clearly with everyone involved in the person's care.

Managing Symptoms and Comfort

Quality end-of-life care focuses on comfort. Key areas include:

  • Pain relief: given regularly and adjusted as needed, following the care plan and clinical guidance.
  • Nutrition and hydration: offering food and fluids in the way the person can manage, without forcing intake — appetite naturally reduces as the body slows down.
  • Positioning and skin care: repositioning regularly to prevent pressure damage and maintain comfort.
  • Mouth care: keeping the mouth clean and moist, which is especially important when a person is eating and drinking less.
  • A calm environment: reducing noise and unnecessary interruptions, and allowing space for family to be present.

Communicating with the Person and Their Family

Honest, gentle communication is central to good care. This means listening carefully, answering questions truthfully and at a pace the person is comfortable with, and never assuming what someone wants to know. Non-verbal communication — a calm tone, a reassuring touch, simply being present — matters just as much as what is said.
