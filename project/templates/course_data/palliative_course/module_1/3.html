Conditions That Commonly Require Palliative Care

Palliative and end-of-life care is not only for people with cancer. It is relevant to a wide range of life-limiting conditions, including:

  • Cancer: particularly advanced or metastatic disease.
  • Heart failure: a progressive condition that can cause repeated hospital admissions.
  • Dementia: in its later stages, when a person becomes increasingly dependent on others.
  • Chronic respiratory disease: such as advanced COPD.
  • Motor neurone disease and other progressive neurological conditions.
  • End-stage kidney or liver disease.

Stages of Terminal Illness

Terminal illness often, though not always, follows a general pattern of stages:

  • Stable phase: symptoms are present but generally well managed, and the person can continue much of their usual routine.
  • Unstable phase: new or worsening symptoms appear and care needs increase.
  • Deteriorating phase: the person's overall condition is declining, with reduced mobility, appetite and alertness.
  • Terminal (dying) phase: the final days or hours of life, when the focus shifts entirely to comfort.

Recognising which phase a person is in helps carers and the wider team adjust the care plan, involve the right specialists, and have timely, honest conversations with the person and their family.

Signs a Person May Be Entering the Terminal Phase

Common signs that death may be approaching within days include:

  • Increasing drowsiness and sleeping for longer periods.
  • Reduced interest in food and drink.
  • Changes in breathing pattern.
  • Cooling and mottling of the skin, particularly in the hands and feet.
  • Withdrawal from conversation and the surrounding environment.

These signs should always be reported to the nurse or clinician in charge — they are not something a care worker should assess or act on alone.

Holistic Caregiving in Practice

As an illness progresses, a person's holistic needs change too. Regular reassessment — not just of physical symptoms but of mood, relationships and what matters most to the person — allows the care team to keep the care plan relevant and person-centred throughout.
