Understanding Grief and Bereavement

Grief is a natural, personal response to loss, and there is no single "correct" way to experience it. Many people find it helpful to think in terms of common reactions rather than fixed stages, including:

  • Shock and denial: difficulty accepting what has happened, even when the death was expected.
  • Anger: directed at the situation, other people, or sometimes at the person who has died.
  • Bargaining and guilt: dwelling on what could have been done differently.
  • Sadness and low mood.
  • Acceptance: gradually adjusting to life without the person.

People can move back and forth between these reactions, and grief has no fixed timescale.

Communicating with Bereaved Families

How a care worker communicates in the hours and days after a death can have a lasting impact on a family. Helpful practices include:

  • Using clear, simple, and kind language rather than euphemisms that could cause confusion.
  • Giving people time and space to react in their own way, without rushing them.
  • Listening more than talking, and not being afraid of silence.
  • Being honest about what you do and do not know.

Practical Support

Families often need practical guidance at a time when they may be too overwhelmed to think clearly. This can include information on:

  • Registering the death and obtaining certificates.
  • Contacting a funeral director.
  • Returning the person's belongings.
  • Who to contact with any further questions.

Signposting to Further Support

Grief can be a long process, and some people benefit from support beyond what family and friends can offer. Care workers should be able to signpost families to bereavement services, such as counselling services, support groups, or their GP, particularly where grief appears to be significantly affecting someone's ability to cope.
